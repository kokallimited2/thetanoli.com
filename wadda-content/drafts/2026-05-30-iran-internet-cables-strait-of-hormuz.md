# Iran Demands Big Tech Pay for Undersea Internet Cables: How VPNs Become Essential for Internet Freedom

## The Internet's Fragile Backbone Is Being Weaponized

There are approximately 530 active submarine communications cables crisscrossing the world's oceans, carrying **99% of all international data traffic**. A single cable — the SEA-ME-WE 5 — carries more data than all the world's satellites combined.

Most people never think about these cables. They just assume the internet works.

That assumption is being tested right now in one of the most strategically important waterways on Earth: the Strait of Hormuz.

In late May 2026, Iran made headlines by demanding that major technology companies — including Google, Meta, Microsoft, and Amazon — pay fees for using undersea internet cables that pass through Iranian territorial waters in the Strait of Hormuz. The stated rationale is that Iran incurs costs for maintaining "telecommunications infrastructure" in the region. Critics see it as a revenue grab and an attempt to exert greater control over internet traffic.

Here's the question that should concern every internet user: **What happens when a country can threaten the physical infrastructure of the internet?**

And the follow-up: **How do you protect your access to the open internet when that happens?**

## How This Affects You — Even If You're Nowhere Near Iran

You might be thinking: "I live in London/New York/Sydney — what does Iran's argument with tech companies have to do with me?"

**More than you'd think.**

### The Cables Everyone's Fighting Over

At least **6 major undersea cable systems** pass through the Strait of Hormuz:

1. **SEA-ME-WE 5** — Connects Singapore to France, passes through Middle East
2. **FLAG Falcon** — Links Europe, Middle East, and India
3. **EIG (Europe India Gateway)** — Connects Europe to India via Middle East
4. **IMEWE (India-Middle East-Western Europe)** — Directly passes through the Strait
5. **SEA-ME-WE 4** — Legacy system still carrying significant traffic
6. **2Africa** (partially) — Massive Meta-led cable system with landing stations in the region

If Iran were to interfere with any of these cables, the impact wouldn't be limited to Iran. It would affect internet traffic between India and Europe, between East Asia and the Gulf, and between Africa and Europe.

### The Precedent Problem

This is the first time a country has demanded direct payment from tech companies for using specific underwater cables. If Iran succeeds, the playbook is simple:
- **Egypt** could do the same for cables passing through the Suez Canal zone
- **Malaysia** for the Malacca Strait
- **Indonesia** for the Lombok Strait
- **Singapore** for the Malacca Strait entrance

Each of these chokepoints carries billions of dollars of internet traffic daily.

### Internet Fragmentation: The Bigger Threat

Iran's cable fee demand fits a broader pattern of **internet fragmentation** — the gradual division of the global internet into national or regional networks with controlled access points. We've seen this in:
- **Russia's "Sovereign Internet" law** (2019) — government-controlled routing and filtering
- **China's Great Firewall** — the most extensive internet censorship system in the world
- **India's repeated internet shutdowns** — 116 shutdowns in 2025 alone
- **Pakistan's VPN blocking** — intermittent VPN bans
- **Turkey's social media throttling** — bandwidth reduction during political events

Iran's cable fee demand adds a new tool to the fragmentation toolbox: **economic coercion through infrastructure control**.

This is the honest question. If Iran physically cuts a cable, a VPN won't fix that — because your traffic still needs to reach a VPN server in another country. **But here's what a VPN CAN do:**

1. **Protect against traffic filtering** — If Iran implements DNS-level or IP-level blocking (as they've done in previous internet crackdowns), a VPN bypasses that by routing your traffic through an encrypted tunnel to servers outside Iran.

2. **Prevent data collection** — Even if traffic passes through monitored infrastructure, VPN encryption means the data collector sees ciphertext, not your actual activity.

3. **Provide route redundancy** — Good VPNs (NordVPN, ExpressVPN) maintain servers in 100+ countries. If one route is degraded, you connect to a different server.

4. **Prevent ISP throttling** — Without VPN, ISPs can detect and throttle VPN-like traffic. With VPN encryption, they can't distinguish your VPN traffic from any other HTTPS connection.

## FAQ

### Will a VPN help if cables are physically cut?

No — a VPN can't replace a severed cable. But a VPN can provide **traffic encryption and route redundancy** in situations where governments use the infrastructure threat to justify new filtering or surveillance measures, which is the more likely scenario.

### Is it legal to use a VPN in Iran?

VPN use for "illegal purposes" is technically illegal under Iran's cybercrime laws, but enforcement varies wildly. During the Strait of Hormuz dispute, enforcement has focused on commercial VPN providers rather than individual users.

### Which VPN is best for circumventing government censorship?

NordVPN (Obfuscated Servers) and ExpressVPN (Lightway Stealth) are the two most reliable choices for censorship circumvention. Both have proven track records in China, Russia, and UAE — the three hardest environments for VPN usage.

### Can Iran really cut the undersea cables?

Physically cutting cables in international waters would be an act of war and is extremely unlikely. The more realistic scenario is Iran using its territorial waters to **inspect, delay, or surcharge** traffic passing through cable landing stations under its jurisdiction.

### What other countries could do this?

Egypt (Suez Canal), Malaysia (Malacca Strait), and Indonesia (Lombok Strait) have the same geographic leverage. None have yet made similar demands, but the Iran case sets a precedent.
